Getting there, and getting back

Lecture 13’s definitions are one line each, and the edge cases are where they bite. A few quick questions on walks and arrows, then lecture’s PageRank example, worked all the way through.

1

Connected only asks for a walk

Nodes a and b are connected if {a, b} is an edge or there’s a walk {a, v1}, {v1, v2}, …, {vk, b} between them. A walk may revisit nodes. A path is a walk whose nodes are all distinct. A closed walk ends where it starts. A graph is connected if every pair of nodes is, and its connected components are its maximal connected subgraphs.

Three quick checks

2

Strongly connected needs a way back

A directed edge (a, b) goes from its tail a to its head b, drawn a → b. Walks and paths carry over, but every step has to follow an arrow. Degree splits into indegree and outdegree. A digraph is strongly connected if for all nodes u and v there’s a directed path from u to v and one from v to u.

Try it

Lecture’s digraph isn’t strongly connected. Add one edge. Which ones fix it?

add
Every edge that works leaves {c, d}. You can walk into {c, d} along (a, c), but no edge leads back out, so nothing in it can reach a or b. Edges among the other nodes, or into c or d, don’t change that. Remember this trap. It’s why §3’s first PageRank has zeros in it.

Two quick checks

3

PageRank flows along links, and pools where there’s no way back

In the web graph, nodes are pages and an edge (a, b) is a link from a to b. Lecture wants a score P(x) for how important page x is, and gets there in three tries:

1st ideaP(x) = indegree(x). Beaten by making lots of dummy pages that link to you.
BetterP(x) = Σv→x P(v). Dummy pages are worth little now, but an important page that links to everyone hands each of them its full weight.
Even betterP(x) = Σv→x P(v) / outdegree(v). Each page splits its weight evenly over its links.

Add one more equation, that the scores sum to 1, and solve.

Try it

Pick an example. Click a page to see where its PageRank comes from, then solve the equations one step at a time. Highlighted edges carry the share of their tail’s PageRank shown on them.

The solution so far
The same equations as a matrix

Without S, a and b get 0. P(a) = P(b) = P(a)/2 forces P(a) = 0. Their weight flows into {c, d} and none comes back. That’s §2’s trap. Lecture says the zeros come from the graph not being strongly connected, and you’ll prove that on the homework.
With S, every page gets something. S links to every page and every page links to S, so the graph is strongly connected. S ranks highest, but it isn’t a real page, so ignore it. Of the real pages c wins, because it collects from a, d and S. In matrix form the equations are MTx = x. Every row of M sums to 1, so MT is a Markov matrix, and linear algebra guarantees a solution with nonnegative entries.
Try it

Put 6300 surfers on the pages, spread evenly. On each click, the surfers on a page split evenly over its links.

The crowd settles on the PageRank. With S it lands on 900, 800, 1400, 1200 and 2000 out of 6300, which is lecture’s 9/63, 8/63, 14/63, 12/63 and 20/63. This is lecture’s random-surfer picture: a page’s PageRank is the share of time a surfer clicking links forever spends there. The edges through S are the surfer getting bored and jumping to a random page. Without S, nobody who reaches {c, d} ever leaves. The two new examples settle on their solved PageRanks too: 200, 100 and 200 out of 500, and 300, 800, 700 and 900 out of 2700.

The short version

  1. Connected only asks for a walk. A walk can revisit nodes and a path can’t. A node with no edges is a component by itself.
  2. In graph induction, start from an arbitrary (n+1)-node graph. Building up never reaches two separate edges, the counterexample to “degree ≥ 1 ⇒ connected”.
  3. Follow the arrows. Every step of a directed walk goes tail to head, and (a, b) and (b, a) are different edges.
  4. Strongly connected needs a way back. If no edge leaves some set of nodes, nothing inside it can reach anything outside it.
  5. Each page splits its PageRank evenly over its links. Where there’s no way back, rank drains to 0. The supernode S makes the graph strongly connected and fixes that.
