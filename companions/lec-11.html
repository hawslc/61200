The picture is not the graph

Most wrong answers about graphs come from trusting the drawing over the definition — where the dots sit, what lines up with what, which side looks bigger. Here are the facts from Lecture 11 that actually decide things, and a lot of small questions that check whether you’re using them.

Definition 1, read literally: a graph G = (V, E) is a non-empty set V of vertices and a set E of 2-element subsets of V. That one sentence rules out self-loops, repeated edges and the empty graph, and it allows graphs with no edges at all. Below, n = |V|.

Degrees
H1Handshake Lemma. Σv∈V deg(v) = 2|E|.
H2So the number of odd-degree vertices is even.
H30 ≤ deg(v) ≤ n − 1, so |E| ≤ n(n − 1)/2, reached exactly by Kn.
B1If G is bipartite with sides L, R: Σv∈L deg(v) = |E| = Σv∈R deg(v).
B2So, given at least one edge, (average degree in L) / (average degree in R) = |R| / |L|.
Coloring
C1χ(G) = k takes two proofs: a proper k-coloring (χ(G) ≤ k) and an argument that no proper (k − 1)-coloring exists.
C2If k vertices are all adjacent to each other, χ(G) ≥ k. So χ(Kn) = n.
C3G is bipartite iff it has a proper 2-coloring: call one color L and the other R.
1

A graph is two sets, and the picture is optional

Definition 1 says G is the pair (V, E), and that’s all it is. Positions, lengths and shapes belong to a drawing, not to the graph. Two very different pictures can be the same graph. Anything you read off a picture, like “those two look adjacent”, has to be checked against V and E.

Is it a graph?

Each of these either satisfies Definition 1 or breaks one specific clause of it. Find the clause.

Definitions differ across sources, and lecture warns you about it. Other books allow self-loops, repeated edges, or a graph with no vertices. In 6.1200 all three are out, and graphs with no edges are in. When a theorem says “for all graphs”, these are the graphs it means.
2

χ(G) takes two proofs, and a coloring is only one of them

Any proper k-coloring proves χ(G) ≤ k. That’s the easy half, and it’s the only half a coloring you found can give you. To prove χ(G) = k you also need χ(G) > k − 1, which means arguing that every attempt with fewer colors fails.

Small questions

3

Induction on graphs starts from the bigger graph

In an induction on |V|, P(n) usually begins “for all n-vertex graphs G …”. So P(n+1) is a claim about every graph with n + 1 vertices. The step has to begin “let G be any graph with n + 1 vertices” and then apply the IH to a smaller subgraph of G, such as G − v, the subgraph of G without one vertex v. If you start from an n-vertex graph and add a vertex, that’s buildup error. It only proves something about the graphs your construction happens to reach.

Try it

Step through both inductive steps

The counterexample was already in §1’s list. Two separate edges, ({1, 2, 3, 4}, {{1, 2}, {3, 4}}): every degree is 1, and it isn’t connected. The bogus step can never produce it, because it only ever attaches a new vertex to a graph that’s already in one piece. Now start from an arbitrary 4-vertex graph G instead and look at G − v. That subgraph can have a vertex of degree 0, so the IH doesn’t apply and the proof gets stuck. For a false claim, that’s the right outcome.

Which opening is right?

4

Bipartite is a fact about the edges, not the layout

Definition 6: G is bipartite if V can be partitioned into L and R so that every edge has one endpoint in each. You get to choose the split. It doesn’t have to be visible in the drawing, and the two sides don’t have to be the same size.

Try it

Pick a graph, then try to sort its vertices into L and R.

To show a graph isn’t bipartite, you have to rule out every split. One split with an edge inside a side shows nothing. The “two columns” graph fails in the columns it was drawn in, and it’s bipartite anyway. What does rule out every split is a ring with an odd number of vertices. Going around it, the sides have to alternate, and an odd number of alternations can’t come back to where it started.

Both sides count the same edges

In a bipartite graph each edge has exactly one endpoint in L. So adding up the degrees on the L side counts every edge once, and so does the R side. Both sums are |E|. Divide each by the size of its side, and the two averages are locked together no matter what the edges are.

Try it

Eight Harvard students, five MIT students. Click a Harvard vertex, then an MIT vertex, to toggle a friendship. Each vertex shows its degree.

The ratio never moves. Once there’s an edge, AM / AH = |H| / |M| = 8/5, however lopsided you make things. That’s how lecture could call the 1994, 2004 and 2007 partner-count studies wrong without looking at their data. The ratio of men’s to women’s averages is pinned at |W| / |M| ≈ 1.03, not 1.74 or 3.33.

Small questions

The short version

  1. Read the graph off V and E, not the picture. Positions, lengths and shapes belong to one drawing. Adjacent means the pair is in E.
  2. Prove χ(G) = k twice. Any coloring you exhibit only gives an upper bound. The lower bound needs its own argument, like a triangle or an odd ring.
  3. In graph induction, start from an arbitrary (n+1)-vertex graph and apply the IH to a subgraph of it. Then check that the subgraph satisfies the IH’s hypothesis.
  4. Bipartite means some split works. One bad split proves nothing, the sides needn’t match in size, and the average degrees are locked at |R| / |L|.
