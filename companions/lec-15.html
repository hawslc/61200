Counting is choices

Lecture’s counting rules come down to one question about each choice: is it an “and” or an “or”? When neither helps, match your set to one you can already size. The relations half of the lecture is in an expandable at the very bottom.

Count by choices: “and” multiplies, “or” adds

Lecture’s shepherd can’t count, but still knows every sheep came back: one pebble per sheep is a bijection, so the sizes match. Counting usually works like that. Describe the set as a series of choices, or match it to a set you can already size.

Try it

X is four Course 6 subjects and Y is three Course 18 subjects. Same two lists, one word different.

Lecture’s version: 10 shirts and 6 pants make 16 articles of clothing, because you choose a shirt or pants, and 60 outfits, because you choose a shirt and pants.

A bijection moves the count

How many subsets do five subjects have? Listing them is tedious, but each subset matches one bit string: bit i says whether subject i is in.

Try it

Click a subject or its bit. You’re changing one choice, written two ways.

Each subset gets exactly one string, and each string comes from exactly one subset. That’s a bijection. By the Bijection rule, there are as many subsets as strings in B⁵ = {0, 1}⁵, and by the Product rule that’s 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 = 32.

The choices can change, as long as their number doesn’t

Rank your top three of five dorms. Your second pick can’t repeat your first, so the plain Product rule doesn’t apply.

Try it

Pick three in order, then start over with a different first pick.

Different first picks leave different sets of four dorms, but always four. That’s all the Generalized Product rule needs. It’s also lecture’s deck of cards: 52 · 51 · ⋯ · 1 = 52! shuffles.
Check

“And” or “or”?

The short version

  1. “And” multiplies, “or” adds. The Sum rule needs pairwise disjoint sets, so don’t count one subject twice.
  2. Use a bijection to move the count. Match your set to one whose size you already know.
  3. Only the number of choices has to stay fixed. The set of choices can change from step to step.
RelationsThe other half of Lecture 15: arrows, the words for counting them, and equivalence relations.

An arrow is one pair

A relation R ⊆ A × B has three parts: a domain A, a codomain B, and a subset of ordered pairs. Each arrow is one pair, drawn: (a, b) ∈ R, a R b, R(a, b), and an arrow from a to b all say the same thing.

The top of your notes says 6.1200J / 18.062J: one subject, two departments. Let A be four subjects, B four Courses, and a R b mean “subject a is offered by Course b”.

Try it

Each square is one element of A × B. Click to add or remove that pair. Exactly one click makes R a bijection. Find it.

These words describe A, B and R together. Lecture’s f(x) = 1/x² isn’t total as ℝ → ℝ but is total as ℝ≠0 → ℝ≠0, with exactly the same pairs. And partial doesn’t mean “not total”: a total function is a partial function too.

Now A is students, B is dorms, and a R b means a lives in b. Each sentence breaks exactly one word. Which?

Three rules turn arrows into blobs

With R ⊆ A × A you have a binary relation on A: one set of dots with arrows among them, which is literally a digraph. Equivalence relations behave like =, and Definition 6 asks for three rules: reflexive, symmetric, transitive.

Try it

A is eight students. Click a rule to see it on the arrows, then draw the relation as blobs. Flip between the two relations.

Check

Each relation on students breaks exactly one rule. Which?

The same contrast with numbers

Take A = {2, 3, …, 12}. It starts at 2 because 1 has no prime factors.

Try it

The two relations sound close. Only one of them can be drawn as blobs.

The pattern to keep. If a R b means f(a) = f(b) for a total function f, all three rules come free, because = already has them. That’s the dorm, the largest prime factor, and a ≡₁₀ b, where f(x) = x rem 10. “Has something in common” comes with no such guarantee: what a shares with b can differ from what b shares with c.

The short version, for relations

  1. Read a pair as an arrow, and say A and B. The same pairs with a different domain or codomain can give different answers.
  2. Count arrows out and arrows in. Function ≤ 1 out, total ≥ 1 out, injective ≤ 1 in, surjective ≥ 1 in.
  3. Ask whether it’s “same value” or “something in common”. The first gives equivalence classes. The second usually fails at transitive.
