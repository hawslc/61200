Ten divisibility facts, and the hypothesis each one is leaning on

Most of these feel obvious. The ones that aren't are the ones with a condition attached — and the condition is usually what a proof forgets to check.

Here's the list. a | b means there is an integer k with ak = b — so 0 | 0, and 5 | 0, but 0 ∤ 5.

D1If a | b, then a | bc for all c.
D2If a | b and b | c, then a | c.
D3If a | b and a | c, then a | sb + tc for all integers s, t.
D4For all c ≠ 0, a | b iff ca | cb.
G1Every common divisor of a and b divides gcd(a, b).
G2gcd(ka, kb) = k · gcd(a, b).
G3If gcd(a, b) = 1 and gcd(a, c) = 1, then gcd(a, bc) = 1.
G4If a | bc and gcd(a, b) = 1, then a | c.
G5If m | a and m | b, then m | gcd(a, b).
G6gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, rem(a, b)).
1

A fact only promises something when its hypothesis holds

Each fact is an implication. When the hypothesis is false, the fact says nothing — the conclusion might happen to be true, or not. So the quickest way to see what a hypothesis is for is to drop it and watch the conclusion fail.

Two of these carry a hypothesis the sheet doesn't print. G2 needs k ≥ 0, because a gcd is never negative. G6 needs b > 0, because the Division Theorem only defines rem(a, b) for b > 0.

Try it

Pick a fact, plug in numbers, and try to make the conclusion fail.

You can't break a fact while its hypothesis holds — try it. Every counterexample you find has a false hypothesis, and the hypothesis that's false is exactly the one doing the work.
G1 and G5 are the same sentence. Rename m to d and they match word for word. In 6.1200 neither one is a theorem — it's part of the definition of gcd (Lecture 08, Definition 3). That clause is why gcd(0, 0) = 0. And G6 is Lecture 08's Lemma 4 with the arguments swapped.
2

Most of the time, one fact does the whole step

Small problems, none needing a calculator. For each one, get the answer, then check which fact got you there — and whether its hypothesis actually held.

Watch problems 6 and 7 together. They look the same: a | bx. Only one lets you cancel the b, and the difference is entirely whether gcd(a, b) = 1.
3

G4's hypothesis is there because Bezout needs a 1

G4 is the fact people most often apply without its hypothesis — “a | bc, and a doesn't divide b, so it must divide c.” The proof shows you exactly where gcd = 1 gets used: you write 1 as an ILC (Bezout), multiply through by c, and use D1 and D3.

Try it

Run the same proof on both examples

The proof doesn't fail at some random step. It fails at the end, where you needed a 1. With gcd 2, Bezout hands you 2 = 6 − 4, and multiplying by 9 only shows 6 | 2 · 9. You can't get rid of the 2. That leftover factor is the gcd, and it's why the hypothesis says gcd = 1.

G3 runs on the same engine. From 1 = sa + tb and 1 = ua + vc, multiply the two together. Every term except one has an a in it, and the last one is tv · bc. So 1 is an ILC of a and bc. Any common divisor of those divides 1 (D3), so the gcd is 1.

Quick check

You know 7 | 10n. A classmate writes “so 7 | n, by G4.” What should they have written first?
This exact move comes back. Lecture 09's theorem that a has an inverse mod n iff gcd(a, n) = 1 is Bezout producing a 1. Recitation 10's CRT uniqueness proof writes 1 = ps + qt and multiplies it through, the same way you just did.
4

The definition decides every edge case

Zero and negative numbers are where “the biggest number that divides both” stops working. 6.1200 avoids the problem by defining gcd(a, b) as the non-negative common divisor that every common divisor divides. When you're unsure about an edge case, go back to that sentence and to “a | b iff ak = b for some integer k”.

Dividing by 0 and dividing 0 are different things. 7 | 0 is true, and so is 0 | 0. What's undefined is rem(a, 0) — and that's why G6 needs b > 0 and Euclid stops when y = 0.
5

The next two lectures keep asking whether gcd = 1

Inverses, cancellation, Fermat, RSA and the CRT all depend on one condition: whether some gcd equals 1. Every question below is a Lecture 08 fact in disguise. The tag on each answer names the fact, and the lecture where it comes back.

The same two arguments keep coming back. To show something is impossible, find a common divisor g > 1 and use D3: everything you can build is a multiple of g. To show something is possible, use gcd = 1 and Bezout: write 1 as an ILC, then multiply through.

The short version

  1. Check the hypothesis before you cite the fact. A false hypothesis means the fact says nothing, not that the conclusion is false.
  2. Know the hidden conditions. G2 needs k ≥ 0, G6 needs b > 0, and D4 says c ≠ 0 for a reason: 0 | 0.
  3. “a doesn't divide b” is not “gcd(a, b) = 1.” 6 ∤ 4, but 6 | 4 · 9 and 6 ∤ 9.
  4. To prove G3 or G4, write 1 as an ILC and multiply through. When the gcd isn't 1, what you get out is the gcd instead of 1, and it doesn't go away.
  5. Settle edge cases from the definition. gcd(0, 0) = 0, gcd(a, 0) = |a|, a gcd is never negative, and 0 is even.
  6. When a later lecture asks whether something is possible, check whether a gcd is 1. A common factor > 1 rules it out (D3); gcd = 1 lets you build it (Bezout).
  7. G1 = G5 = the definition. When a proof needs “the gcd is the biggest,” in 6.1200 it actually means “every common divisor divides it.”
