6.1200 · after Lecture 12 · matching

Stable doesn’t mean happy

Gale-Shapley is quick to learn and easy to run wrong. Here it is on five robots applying for five campus jobs. You call each step before it happens, then see why the result has no rogue couples, and why it’s the best stable outcome for the robots and the worst for the jobs.

It’s fall 2026, and MIT is piloting robot staff. Five humanoid robots are the Applicants and five campus jobs are the Evaluators. Each side ranks the entire other side, with no ties. Every job has an obvious best fit, and not one robot puts its best fit first.

1

Stuck is not the same as biggest

Before anyone ranks anyone, there’s only compatibility: an edge means the robot is qualified for the job. A matching is a set of edges with no shared endpoints. It’s maximal if you can’t add another edge, and maximum if no matching is larger. Those are different things, and the gap between them is easy to fall into.

Try it

Click edges to build a matching. Try to give all five robots a job.

Being stuck is local; being biggest is global. To check maximal, look at each unused edge and see that it touches something already used. To check maximum, you have to rule out every other matching. Lecture’s Claim 1 does that with a degree argument, and the same one works here: Beep is qualified only for Lobby 7 and Dot only for Stata, so a perfect matching must use both of those edges. Axel and Cog have to go somewhere else.
Weighted matchings
Lecture also gives each edge a weight (Definition 5), lower meaning more desirable, and asks for a perfect matching of minimum total weight. That problem and the maximum matching problem both have fast algorithms, beyond the scope of 6.1200. Stable matching asks a different question. It isn’t about how good the total is. It’s about whether anyone would defect.
2

A rogue couple takes two

From here on, every robot is qualified for every job, and each side ranks the other completely. The worry is no longer size. It’s defection. A rogue couple is a robot and a job, not matched to each other, who both prefer each other over their current matches (Definition 7). A matching is stable iff it has none (Definition 8).

The registrar’s first draft just went alphabetically: Axel–Hayden, Beep–Lobby 7, Cog–Media Lab, Dot–Stata, Echo–Z Center. Ringed entries are each party’s current match.

Try it

Rogue or not? Check both sides of each pair.

Click a robot, then a job, to move the robot there. Whoever held that job takes the robot’s old one. Red dashed lines are rogue couples. These preferences have exactly three stable matchings. Find them all.

One-sided envy isn’t instability. Echo would love Media Lab, but Media Lab has Cog, its first choice. Hayden would love Echo, but Echo ranks Hayden last. It takes both. That’s also why stable doesn’t mean happy. Lecture’s Pokémon b and d both had their first choices in a matching that still wasn’t stable, because a and c would rather train together.

Why the two sides matter

Lecture’s Theorem 2: if anyone can be matched with anyone, a stable matching may not exist. a likes b best, b likes c, c likes a, and nobody likes d. Someone has to be stuck with d.

Try it

Pick who gets d. Find the rogue couple.

a: b > c > d
b: c > a > d
c: a > b > d
d: doesn’t matter
Whoever gets d is somebody’s first choice, and that somebody is stuck with its second. The two of them defect together. The three cases are the same up to relabeling, which is why lecture’s proof can open with “WLOG d is matched with a.” With two sides, a stable matching always exists. Gale-Shapley finds one.
3

Applicants walk down their lists, Evaluators trade up

Gale-Shapley runs in days. Every day:

  1. Each Applicant applies to its favorite Evaluator still on its list.
  2. Each Evaluator with more than one applicant keeps its favorite and rejects the rest.
  3. Each rejected Applicant crosses that Evaluator off its list.
  4. If no Evaluator had more than one applicant, stop. Match each Evaluator with its current applicant.

Two things catch people out. A robot that wasn’t rejected applies to the same job again the next day. And a job that keeps a robot can still drop it later.

Run it

Predict each step, then watch it happen.

Count the bumps. Cog bumped Echo, Echo bumped Dot, Dot bumped Beep, and Beep landed at Hayden, the one job nobody had applied to. Every robot finished lower on its list than it started, and every job finished with someone at least as good as anyone it had kept before. Those two one-way facts are most of the proof. (Lecture’s Pokémon run took four days. This one takes five.)
Some books run it one application at a time
Instead of everyone applying each day, one unmatched Applicant applies per step. The order looks like it should matter, but you get the same matching every time. Each version hands every Applicant its optimal match (§5), and there’s only one matching that does that.
What if a robot runs out of jobs?
Lecture’s rule covers it: the Applicant “stays home and is sad.” It never actually happens. That’s Theorem 5, in the next section.

No rogue couples (Theorem 6)

Try it

Pick a pair. Which case kills it?

Look at the shape of each row. Left of the match are exactly the jobs that rejected that robot. Right of it are jobs it never reached. Left of the match, the job prefers its own match (Lemma 8). Right of it, the robot prefers its own match (Lemma 9). No pair is left over to be a rogue couple.
Theorem 5: everyone gets matched
Lecture’s proof, by contradiction. Suppose some Applicant a is unmatched at the end. Then a crossed every Evaluator off its list, so every Evaluator rejected a. An Evaluator that has rejected someone always has an applicant from then on (Lemma 8), so all N Evaluators end up matched. That uses up all N Applicants, a included. ⇒⇐
A tighter bound
Lecture notes that G-S actually always terminates by day (N − 1)² + 1, and usually much faster. For N = 5 that’s Day 17. The main example stopped on Day 5.
5

Whoever applies wins

A job e is a feasible match for robot a if some stable matching pairs them (Definition 9). a’s optimal match is its favorite feasible one, and its pessimal match is its least favorite. The same definitions apply from the jobs’ side. You found all three stable matchings in §2. Here they are again.

Theorems 10 and 11: G-S gives every Applicant its optimal match and every Evaluator its pessimal one. Flip who applies and watch the rings move.

Optimal doesn’t mean first choice. No robot gets its first choice in any stable matching here, because no robot’s first choice is feasible. Optimal means the best you can get without someone defecting. When jobs apply, G-S stops on Day 1, because every job’s first choice is a different robot.

Where this runs for real

Lecture lists the applications: residents and hospitals on Match Day, NYC schools, kidney donation, Akamai servers. The National Resident Matching Program has used an applicant-proposing version since 1998, redesigned by Alvin Roth and Elliott Peranson so that the residents, not the hospitals, are the side that applies. New York City has used deferred acceptance for high-school admissions since 2003. Roth and Lloyd Shapley shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economics; David Gale had died in 2008.

Can anyone game it?
When Applicants apply, no Applicant can get a better match by lying about its list. Telling the truth is always a best strategy. Evaluators sometimes can gain by misreporting, and no stable matching algorithm is immune to lying on both sides at once (Roth, 1982). Interesting, but not part of 6.1200.
The idea behind Theorem 10 (for recitation)
Suppose not, and look at the first day any Applicant is rejected by its optimal Evaluator. The stable matching that makes that Evaluator feasible turns out to contain a rogue couple. Recitation fills in the details and gets Theorem 11 from it.

The short version

  1. Don’t confuse stuck with biggest. Maximal means no edge can be added. Maximum means no matching is larger. You check the first locally; the second needs an argument about every matching.
  2. Check both sides for a rogue couple. Each must prefer the other over its own match. One-sided envy isn’t instability, and unhappy isn’t unstable.
  3. Treat keeping as provisional. Every day, every Applicant applies to the top of what’s left on its list, including the ones kept yesterday, and an Evaluator drops whoever it’s keeping the moment someone better applies.
  4. Prove it with what moves one way. Applicants’ Evaluators only get worse (Lemma 9), Evaluators’ favorites only get better (Lemma 8), and f only drops. Every unmatched pair falls into one of two cases.
  5. Remember who applies. Applicants get their optimal matches and Evaluators their pessimal ones, among stable matchings. That’s not the same as anyone’s first choice.
