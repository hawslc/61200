Three ideas that make induction proofs work

You've argued these at the board. Here they are stripped down to the parts that decide whether a proof actually holds together.

1

A predicate is a sentence

P(n) has to be something that can be true or false. Not a number, not a formula sitting on its own — a sentence with an n in it. Fix n, and you get a claim you could agree or disagree with.

Notice that D(n) is false at n = 2, 3, 4 and true from 5 onward. That's completely fine — a predicate is allowed to be false somewhere. It's just why we'd write the claim as “for all n ≥ 5” instead of “for all n”.

Which of these could you actually run an induction on?

Judge the shape, not whether the claim happens to be true.

The two ways it goes wrong. Write a formula like “P(n) = n(n+1)/2” and P(3) is the number 6 — you can't assume a 6. Write “P(n) ≔ for all n, …” and there's no n left to vary, so P(3) and P(4) say the same thing and the induction can never move.
2

Ordinary vs. strong induction

Both work the same way: prove a starting case, then show how to get from earlier cases to the next one. The only difference is how much you're allowed to assume when you take that step. Ordinary induction gives you P(n) ⇒ P(n+1); strong induction gives you P(0) ∧ P(1) ∧ … ∧ P(n) ⇒ P(n+1).

Try it

What do I get to assume?

Both conditions are separate obligations. Lecture states strong induction as two things you must prove: first that P(0) is true, and second that P(0) ∧ P(1) ∧ … ∧ P(n) ⇒ P(n+1) for every n. The base case doesn't come out of the step — you owe it on its own, just like in ordinary induction.

Now read those dots carefully at small n, because that's where the gap you found in recitation lives. At n = 0, the chain “P(0) ∧ … ∧ P(n)” has collapsed to a single term: the step is just P(0) ⇒ P(1), and P(1) is not something you may assume. The “…” is suggestive, not literal — it stands for however many cases actually exist below the one you're proving, which at the bottom of the induction is very few. Whenever you write the chain, check what it says at your smallest n.

3

Sometimes you have to claim more

Here's the move that feels backwards: your induction gets stuck, so you make the claim harder — and now it works. It makes sense once you remember that the thing you're proving is also the thing you get to assume. A weak claim gives you a weak assumption.

The stronger claim is the one that works. “Less than 1” lets the sum sit right at the edge, so there's no room for the next term. “Exactly 1 − 1/2n” pins down how much room is left — and that's the fact the step needs. You pay by having to prove more; you get paid by having more to work with.

Quick check

Why did claiming more make the proof easier?
You've seen this before. The chocolate-bar predicate had to say “every sequence of splits takes mn − 1” rather than “some sequence does”. Same move: the stronger statement is the one the step can actually use.

The short version

  1. P(n) must be a sentence. If you can't answer “is it true?”, nothing you assume or conclude means anything.
  2. Pick your induction by what the step uses. Only the previous case → ordinary. Anything further back, or something you can't predict → strong.
  3. Read the dots at your smallest n. “P(0) ∧ … ∧ P(n)” collapses to one term at n = 0, and the base case is still yours to prove separately.
  4. Stuck? Try claiming more. A stronger predicate gives a stronger assumption, and that's often exactly what was missing.
